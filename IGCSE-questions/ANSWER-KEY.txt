ANSWERS - SECTION 2 (1 mark each, max 10)
----------------------------------------------------------

Q1  B  Spending more than you planned, or more than your income allows
       LO1 - define overspending
       A thinks any fun spending is overspending.
       C confuses "a want" with overspending - you can buy wants
         inside a budget.
       D confuses frequency with excess.

Q2  B  The school trip you gave up
       LO2 - opportunity cost
       A confuses price with cost.
       C resale value is not what you gave up.
       D ignores the alternative you lost.

Q3  B  Encourage repeated spending because the reward is uncertain
       LO3 - game monetisation
       Uncertain, paid rewards (variable-ratio reinforcement) drive
       repeat purchase. A, C and D are common but wrong beliefs.

Q4  A  Loss aversion and FOMO
       LO3 / LO5 - scarcity
       B is factually backwards. C and D are not scarcity mechanics.

Q5  B  False
       LO5 - sunk cost fallacy
       Choosing "True" IS the sunk cost error: past spending is gone,
       and the decision should depend only on future value.

Q6  A  Anchoring - the higher first price makes HK$699 feel cheap
       LO4 - pricing psychology
       B, C and D name real concepts but none is anchoring.

Q7  C  The two are associated, but the data alone cannot show which
       causes which
       LO6 - correlation is not causation
       A and B each assume a causal direction. D over-corrects: a
       survey can still show a real association.

Q8  A  20%
       LO6 / LO7 - calculating a rate
       400 / 2,000 x 100 = 20%.
       B (25%) comes from dividing by 1,600 instead of income.
       D (40%) uses 800 / 2,000.

Q9  C  Planning a purchase in advance and saving up for it
       LO7 - warning signs
       A, B and D are all recognised warning signs. Planning and
       saving is the healthy behaviour, so it is the exception.

Q10 B  A refund is not guaranteed; it depends on the company's policy
       and the circumstances
       LO8 - local consumer context
       There is no general statutory cooling-off period for online
       purchases in Hong Kong. Refunds depend on platform policy,
       evidence and circumstances. The Consumer Council can mediate
       but cannot order a refund. A and D overstate the protection;
       C is false.

----------------------------------------------------------
SCORING RULES
----------------------------------------------------------

  - 1 mark per correct answer. Maximum 10 on Section 2.
  - "Not sure" = 0 marks, but record the count of "Not sure" answers
    separately. It is a useful confidence measure on its own.
  - Never deduct for a wrong answer.
  - Attitude statements are not scored. Report the percentage who
    agree (A + SA) for each statement, before versus after.

----------------------------------------------------------
CALCULATING KNOWLEDGE GAIN
----------------------------------------------------------

  Report three numbers, not one.

  Raw gain        =  post score - pre score          (out of 10)

  Normalised gain =  (post% - pre%) / (100% - pre%)

      below 0.3      = low
      0.3 to 0.7     = medium
      above 0.7      = high

  Worked example - student 10A-MA07:
      pre 3/10 (30%), post 7/10 (70%)
      raw gain = +4
      normalised gain = (0.70 - 0.30) / (1 - 0.30)
                      = 0.40 / 0.70 = 0.57  -> medium gain

  Report the 3 new-situation questions SEPARATELY from the 10 repeat
  items. Repeat items can be answered from memory; the new ones
  cannot. Strong performance on Q11-Q13 means the learning is real
  rather than remembered.

----------------------------------------------------------
LIMITATIONS TO STATE IN YOUR REPORT
----------------------------------------------------------

  - Test-retest effect: the post repeats the same 10 items, so some
    gain may be memory rather than understanding. The new-situation
    questions are the check against this.
  - Ceiling effect: if the pre-score is already high, raw gain looks
    small. Normalised gain corrects for it.
  - Social desirability: students may agree more in the post
    questionnaire to please the facilitator. If attitudes swing hard
    while knowledge barely moves, suspect politeness.
  - No control group: without a class that did not attend, you
    cannot prove the session caused the change.
  - Unmatched codes: if a student forgets their code, exclude both
    questionnaires from the paired analysis and say how many you
    excluded.

----------------------------------------------------------
A PARAGRAPH THAT WOULD SCORE WELL
----------------------------------------------------------

  "Across 42 matched responses, mean knowledge rose from 34% before
  the session to 71% afterwards, a normalised gain of 0.56 - medium.
  Students were most secure on opportunity cost and pricing
  psychology, and weakest on Hong Kong refund rules, where 38% still
  believed a refund was guaranteed. Only 12 of 42 students answered
  the new-situation questions correctly, which suggests some of the
  headline gain reflects item memory rather than transferable
  understanding. The attitude data supports the knowledge data: the
  share who said they could resist a limited-time offer rose from 31%
  to 58%, while reported post-purchase regret fell from 64% to 47%."

  Adapt these numbers to your own data.
